\section{Background and purpose}
The synchrotron light laboratory Max IV has a unique foundation consisting of $160,000 m^3$ of stabilized clay moraine. In 2010, the first sod was broken for Max IV at Brunnshög in Lund. Facility will be the world's best of its kind. One of the prerequisites for achieving this performance is to reduce the effect of vibrations. The work on the foundation has consisted of reducing the plant's sensitivity to vibrations by increasing the rigidity so that components in accelerators and experimental stations move in time. The foundation also has a certain reducing effect on vibrations from internal and external sources. The goal for vibration is to be below a level of 20-30 nm RMS displacement for frequencies above 5Hz.

In connection with the design of Max IV, a review was made of similar facilities around the world. A very important detail for the performance of the various facilities was related to the vibration characteristics of the facility. In order to achieve a good and cost-effective foundation, a number of simulations were carried out.  The foundation method that proved to work best in resisting both internal and external sources of vibration was stabilization. Beneath the two storage rings, the natural soil is stabilized with lime and slag down to a depth of 4 meters. The stabilization was carried out in layers of 300 mm with an undercut in the underlying layer between 30 and 50 mm to ensure a homogeneous matrix without cracks and joints.

\subsection{Crosshole measurements}
The continuous quality control was performed of each stabilized soil layer. The investigations were performed in the laboratory of Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI) and included seismic measurements and tests on the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) of drill cores. The tests were based on the three core samples collected from the ground surface down to the bottom of the stabilized layer (to 3.15 m). The specimens of drill cores were mapped and photographed, after which selected samples were measured for P-wave velocity, compressive strength, density and deformation. Furthermore, the material samples have been tested at the diffraction beam tube BLI 711 at MAX-lab, Öle Römers väg, Lund.

The crosshole measurements were carried out with a new type of equipment with extended possibilities to measure shear wave and compression wave velocity in the material between the boreholes. The system consists of a transmitter unit that generates shear or compression waves to this there are two receiver units (geophones), see Figure 7.

\begin{figure}[H]
 \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{Fig_07n.jpg}\\
  \caption{Schematic sketch of crosshole measurements on Max IV.}\label{Fig:7n}
\end{figure}

The measurements were performed in accordance with ASTM 4428 - "Standard Test Methods for Crosshole Seismic Testing" (Anon, 2000). As the stabilized material was considered sufficiently strong, no reinforcement of the borehole walls was carried out except by a thin layer of filling on top of the stabilized layer, see Figure 8.

\begin{figure}[H]
 \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{Fig_08n.jpg}\\
  \caption{Crosshole measurements on Max IV. The seismic source was located in borehole No. 1 on the far right of the image. Plastic pipes go down to the stabilized layer. Photo: Per Lindh.}\label{Fig:8n}
\end{figure}

The results from the crosshole measurements show a very good result in terms of both P-wave velocity and S-wave velocity. The design criterion was a minimum shear wave speed (Vs) of 900 m/s and results in Figure 1 show a Vs between 1000 and 1350 m/s (3rd curve from the left, red).

The results from boreholes 2 and 3 show a variation at the depth of 2.5 meters and for borehole 2 also at 3.3 meters. This variation in caliper results is also seen as a change in the hardness of the borehole wall. During the examination of the drill cores, a thin layer, approx. 10 mm, was discovered that had not been stabilized. This corresponds well with the 2.5 meter level. The most likely reason for the variation is that the milling down in the underlying layer was too small and therefore this layer has lower strength. This difference in strength is not visible in all three boreholes and therefore probably has a small extent. The difference in strength does not affect the function of the foundation at the frequencies where the construction is sensitive to vibrations as these wavelengths are much longer (bigger) than this layer.

\section{Laboratory tests}
The investigations were based on three core samples that were carried out from the ground surface down to the bottom of the stabilized layer. The drill cores were mapped and photographed, after which samples were selected for determination of P-wave velocity, compressive strength and density. Furthermore, material samples were tested at the diffraction beam tube BLI 711 at MAX-lab, Öle Römers väg, Lund.

\subsection{Seismic measurements of drill cores}
Evaluating the correlations between the strength properties of the stabilized soil and seismic response of the elastic waves is a key approach in non-destructive measurements of soil strength used in many applications of civil engineering. Given soil specimens are of identical type, the task is to identify the level of resonant frequency in the ultrasonic waves, such that the values of P-wave velocity correspond to the level of strength on each measured sample. In practice, soil is used as a basis for roadbed and foundations which sets up the prerequisites to achieve high performance: stability, strength and reduced effect from vibrations. 

Seismic measurements were performed as a free-free resonant column measurement of the P-wave velocity. Measurements of the test bodies' flex mode were also carried out. The flex mode is more affected by the specimen's boundary conditions and gives a somewhat high determination of the specimen's shear wave velocity. By comparing the resonant frequency longitudinally (P-wave) and the resonant frequency in bending (approximate S-wave), a quality control is obtained on the standard of the specimens in terms of cracks and inhomogeneities in the specimen. A further eight specimens were selected for the determination of uniaxial compressive strength and determination of P- and S-wave velocity. The majority of the test bodies were selected from a depth of 2 to 4 m. The reason for this was that the specimens should have a slenderness factor of 2, i.e., the ratio between length and diameter must be 2. This was chosen to be able to more easily compare seismic and compressive strength results. Since the top two meters of the cores were more cracked, the crack-free length was not 200 mm.

Before the uniaxial pressure test, the resonance frequencies of the specimens were measured in order to compare the results with in situ measurements of P- and S-waves as well as a correlation between P-wave velocity and compressive strength. \subsection{Synchronous light test for qualitative analysis of the mineral composition of the samples}
With the help of X-ray diffraction, the mineral content in crystalline samples can be determined both qualitatively and quantitatively. In this case, where none of the minerals has a known, fixed proportion and thus can function as an internal standard, the analysis can only be of a qualitative nature, i.e., a guess as to what the samples contain, without correct percentages.  

From the drill cores, samples were taken from a superficial layer and one from a deep layer. In total, 4 samples were examined. The measurements were carried out at the diffraction beam tube BLI 711 at MAX-lab, Öle Römers väg, Lund.

The samples were measured high resolution at 0.997Å wavelength from 4 to 40 degree 2 theta angle. The measurements can provide answers to qualitative composition. Via the search/match method, the most important minerals in the samples were identified.  

The purpose was to check whether there were differences in material composition. The samples were run both from natural water content and from oven-dried material. Unfortunately, there were no possibilities to find the pozzellan reaction materials in the data set. This is probably due to too small levels in relation to the high levels of clay minerals. No differences between top and bottom samples could be seen in the test material, see Appendix 2.

\section{Discussion}

\section{Conclusion}
The results from the core sampling and cross hole measurements show good and consistent results. The stabilized foundation well meets the set requirements for a minimum shear wave speed of at least 900 m/s. Furthermore, the investigation shows that the production method has produced a homogeneous monolith without any major deviations in material properties. The method has proven to work very well with regard to the production technology, material technology and economics. No significant differences between superficial and deep-lying samples could be seen in the investigated matrix. Despite variations in the basic material.
